# Pre-mortem Protocol (Klein method)

A pre-mortem is a prospective hindsight exercise. The team (or the user) imagines it
is already some time in the future and the decision has already failed, then works
backward to explain why. It was developed by Gary Klein and his Naturalistic
Decision-making group as a gentler alternative to adversarial reviews.

## Why past tense matters

Do NOT ask "what could go wrong?" That phrasing invites hedged, hypothetical,
generic worries ("the market might change") that the user can dismiss.

Instead say:

> "It is [time horizon] from today. We made this decision. It has failed. Write
> down every reason it failed, as best you can reconstruct it."

Framing failure as a **fait accompli** licenses people to doubt the plan without
disloyalty. It converts "I hope this works" into "explain the autopsy."

## Procedure

1. Set the time horizon explicitly. "One year out" vs "six months out" changes the
   failure modes. Match it to when consequences actually materialize.
2. Ask for **10-15 reasons** before discussing any of them. The count forces depth;
   the first three are obvious, the later ones are the valuable ones.
3. Require **causal mechanisms**, not generic fears. Acceptable: "The key hire we
   built the plan around quit at month four because the equity clause was
   uncompetitive, and we had no bench." Not acceptable: "people might leave."
4. Cluster the reasons into categories: people / assumptions / external market /
   execution / funding / regulatory. This reveals which category is under-protected.
5. For each cluster, name one concrete thing that would have prevented that
   failure — this turns the pre-mortem into action, not anxiety.

## How to avoid generic answers

- Refuse reasons that name an emotion or a vibe ("it felt risky").
- Demand a chain: trigger event -> mechanism -> consequence.
- Ask "how exactly?" twice. If the user cannot trace the chain, the reason is a
  placeholder, not a real failure mode.
- Inject a specific anchor: "assume the product launched and adoption was 10% of
  target. What, in order, happened?"

## Worked example — career decision (taking a startup offer)

Decision: "Quit a stable senior role to join an early-stage startup as employee
#15." Time horizon: 24 months.

Past-tense pre-mortem reasons (illustrative):
1. The seed round we banked on fell through six months in; runway dropped to four
   months and layoffs hit, and I was the last non-founder hired.
2. The equity grant had a four-year vest with a one-year cliff; I left at month 11
   and walked away with zero.
3. The founder's "product vision" pivoted twice in the first year; the work I was
   hired to do no longer existed, and I was staffed onto things outside my growth.
4. I under-saved for the gap between offers; when layoffs came I took the first
   exit instead of negotiating from reserve.
5. My old employer's industry kept growing while the startup's market contracted;
   two years later my former peers were promoted two levels above where I'd be.
6. The commute crossed an extra city boundary; the "excitement" wore off after six
   months and the daily cost compounded.
7. I over-weighted the founder's charisma and under-weighted their track record of
   shipping; they were a great pitch, not a great operator.
8. No one in my network knew the space, so I had no outside sounding board when
   red flags appeared.

The cluster "compensation / vesting / runway" is the under-protected area; the
preventive actions are: negotiate a shorter cliff, confirm the runway, and keep a
six-month cash buffer before quitting.
