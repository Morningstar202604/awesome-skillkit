# Bias-Buster Checklist

Twelve cognitive biases that distort consequential decisions. For each: what it
looks like while the user is deciding, the specific question or action that
neutralizes it, and a short example. The skill asks these out loud; the user must
give real answers, not nods.

## How to use this list

Run the whole list only on hard-to-reverse decisions. On lighter calls, target the
three biases most likely to fire: confirmation, sunk cost, and overconfidence. If
the user can name which bias is pulling them, ask the matching question first.

---

| # | Bias | What it looks like in the decision | Debiasing question / action | Example |
|---|------|------------------------------------|-----------------------------|---------|
| 1 | Anchoring | The first number, first offer, or first option set the range and everything else is judged relative to it | "Forget the first number. What would a neutral, uninformed estimate of this be?" | A salary counter pegged to the recruiter's lowball; reset to market band first. |
| 2 | Confirmation | Only seeking and weighting evidence that the favorite option works; ignoring the rest | "What evidence would DISPROVE my favorite option? Have I actually looked for it?" | Reading only positive reviews of the house; then read the negative ones aloud. |
| 3 | Sunk cost | "I've already spent months/money, so I have to keep going" | "If I were starting fresh today, with nothing already invested, would I choose this?" | A half-built project nobody uses; ask whether you'd start it from scratch now. |
| 4 | Availability | A single vivid, recent story drives the call instead of base rates | "What do the actual statistics / base rates say, not the memorable case?" | Quitting after one viral story about startup success; ask the base failure rate. |
| 5 | Framing | Gain-framed and loss-framed versions of the same choice flip the decision | Reframe it both ways: "maximize upside" vs "minimize what I can't lose" | Same offer read as "a raise" vs "a demotion in disguise." |
| 6 | Overconfidence | "I'm sure it'll work"; timeline and budget treated as certain | Force a pre-mortem: write 10 reasons the estimate is wrong | A three-month plan that quietly assumes nothing goes wrong. |
| 7 | Present bias | Discounting future costs/benefits for immediate gratification | "Future me, one year out — what will they wish I'd done?" | Taking the fun job now and ignoring the resume cost in three years. |
| 8 | Optimism bias | Planning around the best case, not the expected case | Re-plan using the realistic and the worst case; which survives? | Launch plan assuming every task finishes on time. |
| 9 | Survivorship bias | Learning from winners and forgetting the silent failures | "Among everyone who tried this, how many failed, and why don't I hear from them?" | Copying a founder's routine while ignoring identical routines that failed. |
| 10 | In-group / authority | Trusting the opinion of people like us or the senior voice | "What would a skeptical outsider with no skin in the game say?" | Following the lead of the most senior person in the room. |
| 11 | Status-quo bias | "Doing nothing / keeping the current path" feels safer than it is | Treat the status quo as itself a choice, with its own risk | Staying because leaving feels like the risky act. |
| 12 | Narrative fallacy | Weaving the facts into a tidy story that hides uncertainty | "What facts does my story conveniently leave out?" | "This startup is on a rocketship" — a story, not a forecast. |

## The four universal bias-buster questions

When short on time, ask these four — they hit most of the biases above:

1. What would disconfirm my favorite option?
2. What data, if I saw it tomorrow, would change my mind?
3. Who disagrees with me, and what is their strongest argument?
4. What would I tell a close friend who faced this exact choice?

Question 4 is the highest-yield single move: advice to a friend strips away
ego-attachment and often reveals the call the user already knows is sound.
